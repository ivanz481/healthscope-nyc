Long-term healthChronic conditions are often shaped by modifiable risks, early detection, and long-term management.
Prevention is not equally possible for every condition. The guidance below distinguishes between preventing disease, lowering risk, and reducing severe symptoms or complications.
Respiratory health

Asthma

Can it be prevented? Asthma itself cannot always be prevented, but attacks and severe symptoms can often be reduced.

Best-supported steps: identify and avoid personal triggers, avoid tobacco and secondhand smoke, reduce exposure to mold and dust mites when relevant, monitor air quality, follow prescribed controller therapy, and maintain an individualized asthma action plan.

CDC asthma control guidance
Metabolic health

Type 2 diabetes

Can it be prevented? Often, yes—especially when prediabetes is identified early.

Best-supported steps: regular physical activity, healthy eating, and modest weight loss when a person has overweight. CDC's Diabetes Prevention Program uses a goal of about 5%–7% weight loss plus increased activity for people at elevated risk.

CDC diabetes prevention
Cardiovascular health

Heart disease

Can it be prevented? Many cases can be delayed or prevented by addressing major cardiovascular risk factors.

Best-supported steps: do not smoke, stay physically active, eat a heart-healthy diet, maintain a healthy weight, and manage blood pressure, cholesterol, and blood sugar with clinical care when needed.

CDC heart disease prevention
Blood pressure

Hypertension

Can it be prevented? Risk can often be lowered, and existing hypertension can frequently be controlled.

Best-supported steps: regular activity, limiting sodium, avoiding tobacco, maintaining a healthy weight, moderating alcohol, managing stress, checking blood pressure, and taking prescribed medication when lifestyle measures are not enough.

CDC blood-pressure prevention
Shared prevention strategy

The same core habits protect against several chronic diseases

CDC highlights a short list of major modifiable risks shared across many chronic diseases: tobacco use, poor nutrition, physical inactivity, and excessive alcohol use. Preventive care and routine screening also help identify disease earlier, when intervention may have more impact.

CDC chronic-disease prevention
Sources: CDC asthma, diabetes, heart disease, high blood pressure, and chronic-disease prevention guidance. These are general population-health recommendations, not individualized medical advice.
Brain & nervous systemDifferent neurodegenerative diseases have very different causes, so prevention ranges from risk reduction to symptom management.
There is currently no guaranteed way to prevent most neurodegenerative diseases. Where evidence supports it, this section uses the more accurate term risk reduction rather than promising prevention.
Dementia & memory

Alzheimer's disease

What it affects: memory, thinking, behavior, and eventually the ability to perform everyday activities.

Can it be prevented? No strategy is proven to guarantee prevention. However, NIH/NIA identifies several potentially modifiable risk factors associated with dementia.

Risk-reduction priorities: control high blood pressure, stay physically active, avoid smoking, manage diabetes and cholesterol, sleep well, address hearing loss, maintain social connection, and stay mentally engaged.

Reducing severe impact: early evaluation can help identify treatable contributors to cognitive symptoms and may allow earlier use of supportive care or disease-specific treatment when appropriate.

NIA risk & prevention guidance
Movement disorder

Parkinson's disease

What it affects: movement, balance, coordination, and in some people cognition, mood, sleep, speech, or swallowing.

Can it be prevented? There is no proven method that prevents Parkinson's disease.

What may help after diagnosis: regular exercise can improve flexibility, balance, strength, and mobility. Physical, occupational, and speech therapy can help preserve function, communication, and independence.

General health: a balanced diet and adequate fluids can help with issues such as constipation, while medication timing and protein intake may need to be individualized with a clinician.

NINDS Parkinson's overview
Motor neuron disease

ALS

What it affects: motor neurons that control voluntary movement, eventually affecting walking, speaking, swallowing, and breathing.

Can it be prevented? There is no established way to prevent ALS.

Reducing complications: multidisciplinary care, physical and occupational therapy, speech and communication support, nutritional support, respiratory monitoring, and assistive technology can help preserve function and quality of life.

Exercise: appropriately tailored low-impact activity and range-of-motion work may help maintain function, but exercise plans should be individualized to avoid overexertion.

NINDS ALS information
Inherited disease

Huntington's disease

What it affects: movement, thinking, behavior, and emotional regulation.

Can it be prevented? Huntington's disease is caused by an inherited mutation in the HTT gene. There is currently no proven way to prevent disease onset in someone who carries the disease-causing mutation.

What can help: genetic counseling can help families understand inheritance and testing decisions. Once symptoms develop, multidisciplinary care may help manage movement, psychiatric, cognitive, nutritional, and safety concerns.

Research direction: clinical studies are actively testing therapies intended to delay or slow disease onset and progression.

NINDS Huntington's research
Shared brain-health strategy

What is reasonable to do now?

For diseases in which prevention is uncertain, the safest evidence-based approach is to protect overall brain and vascular health: manage blood pressure and diabetes, stay active, avoid smoking, protect hearing, sleep adequately, reduce head-injury risk, stay socially connected, and seek evaluation for persistent neurological changes.

Important distinction

Risk reduction is not a guarantee

Age, genetics, environmental exposures, and still-unknown biological mechanisms play major roles in neurodegenerative disease. A person can follow healthy habits and still develop one of these conditions, while another person with risk factors may never develop it.

That is why this dashboard avoids claims such as “prevent Alzheimer's” or “prevent Parkinson's” when the evidence does not support them.

Sources: National Institute on Aging and National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke. Educational information only; diagnosis, genetic testing, and treatment decisions should be made with qualified clinicians and genetic counselors when appropriate.

Trust layerHow the dashboard sources, validates, refreshes, and falls back when public data changes.
Every metric on this dashboard should have a traceable source, reporting period, and update cadence. Different datasets update on different schedules, so the site keeps those dates separate instead of implying that every number is equally “live.”
Source transparency

Public-health data should be traceable

Respiratory surveillance, vaccination, emergency response, chronic-disease guidance, and neurodegenerative-disease guidance all come from official public-health sources. Each source keeps its own publication schedule and level of certainty.

Current dashboard behavior

Verified values first

The geography section can refresh NYC Open Data 911 data directly. Before live data replaces the current values, the dashboard checks that all five boroughs are present and that incident and response-time values are valid.

If a refresh fails, the site keeps the most recent verified snapshot instead of showing blanks or zeros.

Fallback protection

What happens if a source fails?

If an endpoint is unavailable, blocked, or changes its structure, the dashboard retains the latest verified values. The source status changes to a cached/fallback state instead of silently presenting missing data as current.

01
Reporting periods

Weekly surveillance, monthly emergency-response data, and periodic survey estimates remain on their own timelines.

02
Preliminary data

Some surveillance values can be revised as new records are received. Reporting dates stay visible so users understand the context.

03
Health guidance

Prevention and treatment information comes from CDC and NIH-family agencies and distinguishes prevention from risk reduction and supportive care.

Production architecture

How a fully self-updating version should work

Official sources → scheduled collector → validation → versioned storage → dashboard API → website.

The deployed backend now checks respiratory, vaccination, and 911 sources automatically when the dashboard loads or when users refresh sources. Each endpoint uses cache controls so newly published official values can replace older values without editing the site by hand.

Data-quality notes apply to the current prototype architecture. A production deployment should also log failed updates, preserve historical versions, and expose the exact source URL and retrieval timestamp for every published dataset.
